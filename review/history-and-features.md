## JOB 1. Keysville history and identity

### 1.1 Research notes (verified facts, by topic)

**Founding and name**
- John Key was first licensed to run an ordinary (tavern) on the King's Highway stage road in 1809; he held the license into the early 1820s. The post office opened in 1816 with Key as first postmaster. The Key family sold their property, tavern included, in the 1820s, but the name stuck. The tavern's exact location is unknown. (NRHP nomination, Keysville Historic District, Section 8.)
- Cooperative Living (2008) gives an earlier chain: George Foster bought 134 acres along what is now King Street in 1755; a path through his woods was called the "Kings Highway"; John Key bought the property in 1805 and opened Key's Tavern. The village was once called "Key's Tavern" and "Key's." [verify: 1805 vs. 1809 license date; the nomination is the stronger source]
- SAH Archipedia spells the tavern keeper "John Keys." The nomination, which cites county records, uses "John Key." Use "John Key."
- 1831 description: a tavern, mercantile, boot and shoe factory, two blacksmiths, two wagon makers and seven houses. (Nomination.)

**Railroad**
- The Richmond and Danville Railroad completed track through Keysville in 1853 and built the first depot that year. SAH Archipedia dates the railroad's arrival to November 1852. [verify: 1852 vs. 1853; the nomination and DHR both say 1853]
- A branch toward Clarksville (Richmond and Mecklenburg Railroad) was started before the war. Confederate authorities took up its rails early in the war and used the material for the Piedmont Railroad. The Richmond and Mecklenburg was chartered in 1875 and completed Keysville to Clarksville (31 miles) in 1884. (Nomination; virginiaplaces.org.) DHR's station page says an 1882 spur toward Durham, NC put the depot at a "Y" junction. [verify: 1882 vs. 1884 completion]
- The 1893 USGS Farmville quadrangle shows the depot sitting inside the junction of the Richmond and Danville and Richmond and Mecklenburg tracks. (virginiaplaces.org.)
- Note for the client: there was never a "Keysville and Virginia Beach Railroad." The junction line was the Richmond and Mecklenburg (Keysville to Clarksville, later extended through Oxford toward Durham), leased to Southern Railway in 1898. Southern took over the Richmond and Danville main line in 1894. Norfolk Southern formed in 1982; the Keysville branch was leased to short lines (Virginia Southern, 1988; Buckingham Branch since 2009). The main line still carries freight. (virginiaplaces.org; nomination.)
- In the mid-1890s twelve passenger trains a day served Keysville; a 1907 bank advertisement counted 11 inbound and 11 outbound mail trains daily. (Nomination.)
- The last passenger train stopped at Keysville in 1956. (Nomination; DHR.)

**The depot**
- Built in stages 1890 to 1900 by the Richmond and Danville on the site of the antebellum depot burned in the Civil War. One-story frame building, offices and segregated waiting rooms at the north end, freight room with docks on three sides to the south. Listed on the Virginia Landmarks Register 18 Sept 2008 and the National Register 12 Nov 2008 (ref. 08001050). Used as a stand-in for stations in Eastport, Maine and Warm Springs, Georgia in the 1976 TV film "Eleanor and Franklin." (Wikipedia; DHR.)
- Norfolk Southern gave the depot to the Friends of the Fort Mitchell and Keysville Depots in 2006. In October 2013 the group received an $81,000 VDOT grant for roof, deck and freight-room work, with a $21,000 local match, and planned a community center for farmers' markets and gatherings. (Cooperative Living 2008; WSET 2013.)
- Style Weekly (Aug 2021) described the depot as still standing and "awaiting restoration."
- DHR's listing page (updated 17 Sept 2026) now files the station under "Virginia's Lost" delisted landmarks: "By the early 2020's the Keysville Railroad Station was no longer extant." Delisted from the VLR 17 Sept 2026. No cause or date is given. [verify locally: what happened to the depot, and when; our copy should not promise a standing depot]

**Civil War and Reconstruction**
- In 1864 Union Gen. James Wilson's cavalry occupied the town for several nights, destroyed the Richmond and Danville track and depot, and burned several houses, a saddle shop and the Masonic lodge where Confederate supplies were stored. (Nomination.) This matches the Wilson-Kautz Raid of June 1864, which ended at the Battle of Staunton River Bridge in Charlotte and Halifax counties. (Wikipedia, Charlotte County.)
- Style Weekly states that Jefferson Davis and the Confederate cabinet passed through Keysville in April 1865 on the Richmond and Danville line to Danville. [verify: plausible, since the R&D main line ran through Keysville, but single secondary source]
- The railroad rebuilt the depot as early as 1866. An 1874 account lists two stores, a tannery, a blacksmith and wheelwright shop, the Keysville Hotel run as a boarding house, the rebuilt depot and about a dozen houses. In 1874 the 300-acre "Keysville Property tract" was bought by Ohio investors Michael Hardman and Frederick Rummell, who platted streets and lots. First school (Shotwell Institute) built 1881. A tornado wrecked the young business block on the south side of King Street in 1882. (Nomination.)
- In 1869 Joseph Holmes, a politically active freedman, was murdered on the steps of the Charlotte County courthouse; four men were indicted but never tried. (Nomination.)

**Incorporation and boom**
- The General Assembly passed the act incorporating the Town of Keysville on 24 May 1887. Less than a month later a fire that began in a tobacco warehouse destroyed two general stores, two barrooms and the rebuilt Masonic lodge. (Nomination.)
- Ash Camp Baptist Church (founded 1802) built a new meeting house near town in 1857-58 (builder Josiah Dabbs, $3,000); it is believed to be the oldest building standing in the historic district and renamed itself Keysville Baptist Church in the late 1880s. (Nomination.)
- Keysville first appeared in the federal census in 1890 with 126 residents. In 1891 the Virginia Land Improvement Company replatted the town for 10,000 people. Fires hit the business district in 1892, 1893 and twice in three days in 1897. Gold finds near town were reported in 1890 and 1894; no significant mining followed. A communal well was set in the middle of King Street in November 1896. (Nomination.)
- Jesse H. Wilson, born enslaved in Charlotte County in 1856, opened a business about 1880, lost his first building to fire in 1887, and in 1893 built the two-story brick Wilson & Co. general store on Railroad Avenue (general store, barber shop, shoe shop and funeral home). It is the oldest commercial building in town. He helped found the Keysville Mission Industrial Academy (later Bluestone-Harmony Academic and Industrial School) in 1898, a private Black Baptist boarding school on Bluestone Road with five teachers and more than 200 students; it closed in 1957. Wilson died in 1925; his family ran the store until the 1990s and the Wilson Funeral Home still operates. (Nomination; Wikipedia, Keysville Mission Industrial Academy.)
- Hotel Charlotte, Railroad Avenue, built 1907 by Margaret Hanmer Wade and her brother Alexander "Bate" Hanmer, directly across from the depot; local tradition says conductors wired dinner reservations ahead. Converted to apartments by 1944. (Nomination.)
- "Keysville Tonic Springs," about a mile out on Bluestone Road, operated as a health resort for at least seven years around 1907. (Nomination.)
- In 1913 the Hanmer Motor Car Company assembled cars shipped in by rail; Hanmer Ford built a two-story showroom on King Street in 1923. Jack Brooks arrived in 1914, converted the ice plant to diesel and strung electric wire through town, making Keysville the only town in Charlotte County with electric lights at the time. (Nomination.)
- Tornado 1915 destroyed the Episcopal church; the "Little Briery" church rebuilt on the site is now the Keysville Library. Fires in 1927, 1929 and 1933 (the last took the Bank of Keysville and the Hanmer hardware store; the rebuilt hardware is now Rebel Sporting Goods, 115 King Street, and the New Theatre opened 1937 in part of the ruin). The town built its water and sewer system and first volunteer fire department in the 1930s. (Nomination.)
- Virginia Crafts, Inc. built a tufted-rug plant west of the tracks in 1947; its president was John H. Daniel (1896-1972), a Charlotte County farmer and businessman who served in the House of Delegates 1944-1969 and chaired Appropriations. The Southside Virginia Community College campus outside town is named for him. (Nomination; Virginia House of Delegates history site.)
- Charlotte Lodge No. 146 on Wilson Street, built 1958, closes the historic district's period of significance (1857-1958). (Nomination.)

### 1.2 (a) "Our story" (English, about 400 words)

Keysville started with a tavern. In 1809 John Key took out a license to keep an ordinary on the King's Highway, the stage road that ran through Charlotte County. In 1816 the village got a post office, and Key was its first postmaster. His family sold out in the 1820s, but the name stayed. In 1831 the whole place was a tavern, a store, a boot and shoe shop, two blacksmiths, two wagon makers and seven houses.

The railroad changed that. The Richmond and Danville line reached Keysville in 1853 and built a depot. Tobacco farmers from three counties could now sell their crop here and ship it to market, and the warehouses, stores and hotels followed. The Civil War took most of it back. In 1864 Union cavalry occupied the town, tore up the track and burned the depot, the Masonic lodge and several houses. Recovery was slow. An 1874 count found two stores, a tannery, a boarding house and about a dozen homes.

The town was incorporated on May 24, 1887. A fire burned the center of town less than a month later. That became a pattern. Fires in 1892, 1893, 1897, 1927, 1929 and 1933 each took a block, and each time people rebuilt in brick. By the 1890s twelve passenger trains a day stopped at the depot. A branch line to Clarksville made Keysville a junction. Four hotels served rail travelers, and the Hotel Charlotte, built in 1907 across from the tracks, still stands on Railroad Avenue.

The oldest store in town was built by Jesse H. Wilson, who was born enslaved in Charlotte County in 1856. His 1893 brick building on Railroad Avenue held a general store, a barber shop, a shoe shop and a funeral home. He preached, bought land and helped start the Bluestone-Harmony school in 1898, which sent Black students to colleges across the South for nearly sixty years. The Wilson Funeral Home is still in business.

Keysville had electric lights before any other town in the county, thanks to Jack Brooks and a converted ice plant in 1914. A 1928 newspaper ad counted four department stores, three millinery shops, three automobile dealers and one dentist, and told readers to shop at home.

The last passenger train left in 1956. The town turned to face King Street and the highways, and it kept going. Today about 800 people live here. Freight trains still roll through. The historic district was listed on the National Register in 2023. The college campus named for John H. Daniel sits just outside town. The water tower still marks the center, and King Street still has stores in the brick buildings that were built after the fires.

### 1.2 (b) Timeline

1. **1809** — John Key is licensed to keep an ordinary on the King's Highway stage road. The village that grows around it takes his name, and in 1816 he becomes its first postmaster.
2. **1853** — The Richmond and Danville Railroad lays track through town and builds the first depot. Tobacco warehouses follow.
3. **1857-58** — Ash Camp Baptist Church, founded in 1802, builds a new frame meeting house near town for $3,000. It is believed to be the oldest building in the historic district.
4. **1864** — Union cavalry under Gen. James Wilson occupy Keysville, destroy the railroad and depot, and burn houses, a saddle shop and the Masonic lodge.
5. **1874** — Ohio investors buy the 300-acre Keysville tract and lay out streets and lots. The town's first school, the Shotwell Institute, opens in 1881.
6. **May 24, 1887** — The General Assembly incorporates the Town of Keysville. A fire destroys much of the business center within a month.
7. **1893** — Jesse H. Wilson, born enslaved in 1856, builds the brick Wilson & Co. store on Railroad Avenue. It is still the oldest commercial building in town.
8. **1890-1900** — The railroad builds the frame depot that stood for more than a century. Twelve passenger trains a day stop in Keysville.
9. **1898** — The Keysville Mission Industrial Academy, later Bluestone-Harmony, opens on Bluestone Road for Black students. It closes in 1957.
10. **1907** — The Hanmer family opens the Hotel Charlotte across from the depot. Conductors wired dinner orders ahead from the train.
11. **1914** — Jack Brooks converts the ice plant and wires the town. Keysville becomes the only town in the county with electric lights.
12. **1956** — The last passenger train stops at Keysville. Freight service continues.
13. **1971** — Southside Virginia Community College opens the John H. Daniel Campus on Daniel Road.
14. **April 28, 2023** — The Keysville Historic District is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

### 1.2 (c) "Only in Keysville" cards

1. **A town that burned and rebuilt six times.** Fires in 1887, 1892, 1893, 1897, 1927, 1929 and 1933 each took part of King Street. The brick storefronts you see today are the answer.
2. **The oldest store was built by a man born enslaved.** Jesse H. Wilson put up the Wilson & Co. building on Railroad Avenue in 1893. His family ran it until the 1990s, and the funeral home he started is still open.
3. **First electric lights in Charlotte County.** In 1914 Jack Brooks converted a steam ice plant to diesel, hooked up a generator and wired the town, house by house.
4. **A hotel that took dinner orders from the train.** The Hotel Charlotte, built in 1907 across from the depot, still stands. Conductors wired passengers' orders ahead so supper was hot when the train pulled in.
5. **Hollywood came to the depot.** The 1890s frame station stood in for stations in Maine and Georgia in the 1976 TV film "Eleanor and Franklin," which won 11 Emmys.
6. **Gold fever, 1890.** Newspapers reported gold strikes within five miles of town in 1890 and 1894. No mine ever paid, but the talk helped set off a building boom.
7. **A well in the middle of King Street.** In November 1896 the town dug a public well in the middle of its main street for the farmers who drove in each week.
8. **Where Patrick Henry rests.** Red Hill, Henry's last home and grave, is in the same county, about 25 miles southwest. Congress made it a National Memorial in 1986.

### 1.2 (d) Spanish translations

**(a) Nuestra historia**

Keysville comenzó con una taberna. En 1809, John Key obtuvo una licencia para administrar una posada en el King's Highway, el camino de diligencias que atravesaba el condado de Charlotte. En 1816 el pueblo recibió una oficina de correos, y Key fue su primer administrador. Su familia vendió la propiedad en la década de 1820, pero el nombre permaneció. En 1831 todo el lugar consistía en una taberna, una tienda, una zapatería, dos herrerías, dos talleres de carretas y siete casas.

El ferrocarril cambió todo eso. La línea Richmond and Danville llegó a Keysville en 1853 y construyó una estación. Los cultivadores de tabaco de tres condados podían ahora vender su cosecha aquí y enviarla al mercado, y siguieron los almacenes, las tiendas y los hoteles. La Guerra Civil se llevó casi todo. En 1864 la caballería de la Unión ocupó el pueblo, levantó las vías y quemó la estación, la logia masónica y varias casas. La recuperación fue lenta. Un recuento de 1874 registró dos tiendas, una tenería, una casa de huéspedes y cerca de una docena de viviendas.

El pueblo se incorporó el 24 de mayo de 1887. Menos de un mes después, un incendio consumió el centro. Eso se convirtió en un patrón. Los incendios de 1892, 1893, 1897, 1927, 1929 y 1933 destruyeron una manzana cada vez, y cada vez la gente reconstruyó en ladrillo. Para la década de 1890, doce trenes de pasajeros al día paraban en la estación. Un ramal hacia Clarksville convirtió a Keysville en un empalme ferroviario. Cuatro hoteles atendían a los viajeros, y el Hotel Charlotte, construido en 1907 frente a las vías, sigue en pie en Railroad Avenue.

La tienda más antigua del pueblo fue construida por Jesse H. Wilson, nacido en esclavitud en el condado de Charlotte en 1856. Su edificio de ladrillo de 1893 en Railroad Avenue albergaba una tienda general, una barbería, una zapatería y una funeraria. Predicaba, compraba tierras y ayudó a fundar la escuela Bluestone-Harmony en 1898, que durante casi sesenta años envió estudiantes negros a universidades de todo el Sur. La funeraria Wilson sigue en funcionamiento.

Keysville tuvo luz eléctrica antes que cualquier otro pueblo del condado, gracias a Jack Brooks y a una planta de hielo reconvertida en 1914. Un anuncio de periódico de 1928 contaba cuatro grandes almacenes, tres sombrererías, tres concesionarios de automóviles y un dentista, y pedía a los lectores que compraran en el pueblo.

El último tren de pasajeros partió en 1956. El pueblo se volvió hacia King Street y las carreteras, y siguió adelante. Hoy viven aquí unas 800 personas. Los trenes de carga todavía pasan. El distrito histórico fue inscrito en el Registro Nacional en 2023. El campus universitario que lleva el nombre de John H. Daniel está a las afueras del pueblo. La torre de agua todavía marca el centro, y King Street todavía tiene comercios en los edificios de ladrillo que se levantaron después de los incendios.

**(b) Cronología**

1. **1809** — John Key recibe licencia para administrar una posada en el camino de diligencias King's Highway. El pueblo que crece a su alrededor toma su nombre, y en 1816 Key se convierte en su primer administrador de correos.
2. **1853** — El ferrocarril Richmond and Danville tiende vías por el pueblo y construye la primera estación. Le siguen los almacenes de tabaco.
3. **1857-58** — La iglesia bautista Ash Camp, fundada en 1802, construye un nuevo templo de madera cerca del pueblo por 3,000 dólares. Se cree que es el edificio más antiguo del distrito histórico.
4. **1864** — La caballería de la Unión bajo el general James Wilson ocupa Keysville, destruye el ferrocarril y la estación, y quema casas, una talabartería y la logia masónica.
5. **1874** — Inversionistas de Ohio compran el terreno de 300 acres de Keysville y trazan calles y lotes. La primera escuela del pueblo, el Instituto Shotwell, abre en 1881.
6. **24 de mayo de 1887** — La Asamblea General incorpora el Pueblo de Keysville. En menos de un mes, un incendio destruye gran parte del centro comercial.
7. **1893** — Jesse H. Wilson, nacido en esclavitud en 1856, construye la tienda de ladrillo Wilson & Co. en Railroad Avenue. Sigue siendo el edificio comercial más antiguo del pueblo.
8. **1890-1900** — El ferrocarril construye la estación de madera que se mantuvo en pie durante más de un siglo. Doce trenes de pasajeros al día paran en Keysville.
9. **1898** — Abre en Bluestone Road la Keysville Mission Industrial Academy, después llamada Bluestone-Harmony, para estudiantes negros. Cierra en 1957.
10. **1907** — La familia Hanmer abre el Hotel Charlotte frente a la estación. Los conductores telegrafiaban los pedidos de cena desde el tren.
11. **1914** — Jack Brooks reconvierte la planta de hielo y electrifica el pueblo. Keysville se convierte en el único pueblo del condado con luz eléctrica.
12. **1956** — El último tren de pasajeros para en Keysville. El servicio de carga continúa.
13. **1971** — Southside Virginia Community College abre el campus John H. Daniel en Daniel Road.
14. **28 de abril de 2023** — El Distrito Histórico de Keysville se inscribe en el Registro Nacional de Lugares Históricos.

**(c) Solo en Keysville**

1. **Un pueblo que se quemó y se reconstruyó seis veces.** Los incendios de 1887, 1892, 1893, 1897, 1927, 1929 y 1933 destruyeron parte de King Street cada vez. Las fachadas de ladrillo que se ven hoy son la respuesta.
2. **La tienda más antigua la construyó un hombre nacido en esclavitud.** Jesse H. Wilson levantó el edificio Wilson & Co. en Railroad Avenue en 1893. Su familia lo administró hasta la década de 1990, y la funeraria que fundó sigue abierta.
3. **La primera luz eléctrica del condado de Charlotte.** En 1914, Jack Brooks convirtió una planta de hielo a vapor en diésel, conectó un generador y electrificó el pueblo, casa por casa.
4. **Un hotel que tomaba pedidos de cena desde el tren.** El Hotel Charlotte, construido en 1907 frente a la estación, sigue en pie. Los conductores telegrafiaban los pedidos de los pasajeros para que la cena estuviera caliente al llegar el tren.
5. **Hollywood llegó a la estación.** La estación de madera de la década de 1890 representó estaciones de Maine y Georgia en la película para televisión de 1976 "Eleanor and Franklin," que ganó 11 premios Emmy.
6. **Fiebre del oro, 1890.** Los periódicos reportaron hallazgos de oro a menos de cinco millas del pueblo en 1890 y 1894. Ninguna mina resultó rentable, pero los rumores impulsaron un auge de la construcción.
7. **Un pozo en medio de King Street.** En noviembre de 1896, el pueblo excavó un pozo público en medio de su calle principal para los agricultores que llegaban cada semana.
8. **Donde descansa Patrick Henry.** Red Hill, la última residencia y tumba de Henry, está en el mismo condado, a unas 25 millas al suroeste. El Congreso lo declaró Monumento Nacional en 1986.

1. **King Street brick row, south side, looking west** from in front of the former Hanmer Ford building (the two-story brick at the east end). Shoot early morning on a weekday with low sun raking the facades; match the framing of the 1943 Library of Congress photo. Landscape, hero width.
2. **Hotel Charlotte, west elevation, from across the tracks on Railroad Avenue**, with rails in the foreground. Late afternoon, sun on the facade. Confirm no train is due; stay off the right-of-way.

7. **Korean-Vietnam War Memorial Park, King Street**: the granite markers and the Blue Star Highway marker, shot low and close enough to read the inscriptions. Soft light, early evening.

## JOB 2. What the best municipal sites do, and what a small town can borrow

### 2.1 What the evidence says residents actually use

- Granicus's top-tasks study of 59 city sites (Google Analytics, top 40 pages each) found parks and recreation info is the number one task on every device (15% desktop, 32% phone), followed by job openings, paying utilities, events/meetings/minutes, and waste and recycling schedules. 49% of traffic was mobile. Phones are used for quick lookups (trash day, who to call, events); desktops for permits, planning documents and bids.
- Vision Internet's UX ranking: pay utility bill, job openings, pet adoption, book a facility, permits, agendas and minutes, licenses, parking tickets, bids, GIS maps.
- Boston.gov's most-visited page is an address lookup (Assessing Online, about 3,000 visits a day); the team's lesson is that a single address field can route to everything tied to a home (trash day, polling place, precinct). Boston's content model is "how-tos" (every way to complete a task on one page, as tabs) and "articles" in plain language, each with the responsible department's contact near the top. User testing of a department homepage found announcements distracting and long link lists cluttered; the fix was separate pages for events, places and partnerships.
- Asheville (beta 2019) and San Rafael (digital overhaul) both reorganized around services rather than departments, used analytics plus interviews with counter staff, put a feedback button on every page, and barred departments from writing their own content strategy.
- Center for Digital Government 2025 Digital Cities winners in the under-75,000 class: Tamarac FL, Marietta GA, Gainesville GA, Coral Gables FL, Tinley Park IL, Danville VA, Punta Gorda FL, Newark CA, Delray Beach FL, Kalamazoo MI, Williamsburg VA, Dunwoody GA. Lynchburg and Roanoke tied 7th in the 75,000-124,999 class. Themes: omnichannel contact centers, real-time data, smart parks. Danville and Williamsburg are the nearest Virginia models to study.
- Small-town calendar policies worth copying (Bellows Falls VT; Hutto TX; Grand Terrace CA): events must be public and in town; no political or purely commercial listings; organizer name, email and phone required; staff may edit for length and clarity; 3 to 5 business day review; nothing more than 12 months out; a disclaimer that the town does not endorse or verify listings; cancellations must be reported. Bellows Falls requires a square 1000x1000 image with alt text and legible phone-size text.
- Town photo calls (Walpole MA, Yorktown NY, Chelmsford MA, Blackstone MA): minimum width (Walpole: 1950 px), photographer credit, and use on the town site and annual report. Most lack explicit rights language; ours should have it.

### 2.2 Fifteen features worth adding to a small-town site (promote community and local business, rarely present)

Each: what it is / why residents use it / how to build it simply with static data and JavaScript.

1. **Community event calendar with public submissions and photos.** Residents, churches, the fire department and businesses post their own events. It is the one page every household checks and the one thing most small towns outsource to Facebook. Build: a `data/events.json` rendered client-side into month and list views (ICS export per event); a submission form (our forms plugin) that emails staff and writes to a "pending" collection; a one-click approve step publishes to the JSON. Rules on the form itself: public events inside town or benefiting a town group; no campaign or sales-only listings; organizer contact required; review within 3 business days; listings may be edited for clarity; nothing more than 12 months out; cancellations must be reported; town does not endorse or verify. Photos: optional, one image, JPG or PNG, minimum 1200 px wide, cropped to 3:2 server-side; the submitter must own the photo or have permission, check a box granting the town a non-exclusive license to display and credit it, and confirm no one under 18 is identifiable without parental consent; required alt text field with a 125-character hint ("describe what is in the picture, not the event"); text-on-image flyers are rejected or accompanied by the same text in the description so screen readers get it; automatic photographer credit line.

2. **"Shop Keysville" business showcase with owner stories.** A card grid of every local business (name, hours, phone, map pin, category filter), with a longer profile for owners who sit for a five-question interview and one portrait. Residents use it to find who is open and when; visitors use it before they stop. Build: `data/businesses.json` plus one Markdown-to-JSON story per profiled business; filterable grid, "open now" badge from stored hours, JSON-LD LocalBusiness for each card. A "claim or update my listing" form routes to staff. Rotate a featured owner on the home page monthly.

3. **"Keysville in pictures" photo wall with resident submissions.** A masonry gallery of town photos by season and place, each with photographer credit and alt text, with a yearly "best of" that goes on the home hero. Residents submit from their phones; the town gets real photos instead of stock. Build: submissions through the form plugin into a pending folder; staff approve and the build script resizes to 3 widths (480/960/1600), strips EXIF location data, writes `data/photos.json`. Alt text required at submission and edited by staff; captions distinct from alt text; keyboard-navigable lightbox with Escape to close and focus return. Rights: non-exclusive, perpetual license to the town for web, print and social, credit always shown, submitter may request removal; no identifiable minors without consent.

4. **New resident welcome guide.** One page that answers the first thirty days: water and sewer sign-up, trash and recycling days, the county dump, the library card, where to register to vote, school enrollment, the post office, the volunteer fire department, where to get a dog license, who plows the roads (VDOT vs. town). Residents use it when they move in and share it with the next person. Build: a single HTML page from `data/welcome.json` with a printable checklist (CSS print stylesheet) and a Spanish toggle; include a downloadable one-page PDF.

5. **Volunteer and board vacancy board.** A list of open seats (planning commission, BZA, parade committee) and volunteer needs (fire department, library friends, cleanup days) with what the role is, time commitment, meeting schedule and a one-click "I'm interested" form. Residents rarely know these seats exist. Build: `data/vacancies.json` with open/closed state and deadline; form routes to the clerk; closed items drop off automatically by date.

6. **Self-guided history walk.** Ten numbered stops from the depot site along Railroad Avenue and King Street (Wilson & Co., Hotel Charlotte, the brick row, the library, the memorial park, the churches, the water tower), each with a then-and-now photo, two paragraphs, and audio. Shareable by QR codes on small sidewalk plaques. Residents bring visiting family; schools use it. Build: `data/tour.json`, a Leaflet map with static tiles or a simple SVG map, geolocation "nearest stop" button, printable PDF, `<audio>` elements with transcripts.

7. **Address lookup: "What's my day?"** One field. Type a street address and get trash and recycling day, bulk pickup, polling place, council ward, the nearest hydrant plan and snow route. Boston's single most-used page is an address lookup; this is the small-town version. Build: `data/streets.json` keyed by street name with ranges; pure client-side match; falls back to a phone number if no match.

8. **Plain-language "How do I" index.** Thirty tasks phrased the way people say them ("pay my water bill," "report a street light out," "rent the community building," "get a yard sale permit"), each a short page listing every way to do it (online, phone, in person) with the responsible contact at the top. Boston's how-to pattern. Build: `data/howto.json`, search-as-you-type over titles and synonyms, each page one template.

9. **Lost and found pets, and other hyperlocal notices.** A moderated board for lost pets, found keys, free firewood, yard sales, with photo and a 14-day auto-expiry. This is what residents use Facebook groups for; the town can host it with less noise. Build: same submission pipeline as events; `data/notices.json`; expiry enforced at build time.

10. **Meeting agendas, minutes and a plain-English "what happened" digest.** Agenda packets as accessible PDFs plus a three-bullet summary of each council meeting, posted within 48 hours, and a "decisions" tag so residents can see votes without reading minutes. Build: `data/meetings.json`, one list page with filters, ICS for upcoming meetings, and an email/SMS alert signup through the forms plugin.

11. **Town alerts bar with SMS/email signup.** A single dismissible bar at the top for water main breaks, boil notices, road closures and office closures, backed by an opt-in list. Residents rank alerts and trash info in the top five everywhere. Build: `data/alert.json` with start/end time; the bar shows only when current; signup form writes to the list; the same JSON feeds a public `/alerts.rss`.

12. **Jobs and bids, local first.** Town openings plus a free listing for local employers (the plant, the college, the orchard) and open bids for town work, with closing dates. "Look for a job" is the second task on every device in the Granicus data. Build: `data/jobs.json` with employer, close date, how to apply; expired items hide automatically; employers submit through the form.

13. **Facilities and rentals with live availability.** The community building, pavilion, ball field and library room: photos, capacity, rules, fee, and a calendar of booked dates with a request form. Booking a facility is in the national top four. Build: `data/facilities.json` plus a bookings calendar fed from the approved events file; request form to the clerk.

14. **Faith, clubs and civic groups directory.** Every church, scout troop, Ruritan, VFW, 4-H and garden club with meeting time, contact and a line about what they do. New residents and newcomers to town cannot find these anywhere. Build: `data/groups.json`, same card component as the business grid, "update my listing" form.

15. **Feedback on every page, and a public "you said, we did" list.** A one-line "Was this page helpful?" with an optional comment, plus a quarterly posted list of changes made because of feedback (Asheville and San Rafael both do this). Build: a tiny form that posts to the forms plugin with the page URL attached; staff publish `data/changes.json`.

Accessibility and build notes that apply to all of the above: every user-submitted image needs alt text at submission and a staff edit before publish; captions and alt text are different fields; all JSON renders to real HTML lists and headings (no div soup) so screen readers and search engines read the same thing; every form is the Pascat forms plugin shortcode with per-form destinations (clerk, mayor, fire chief); all dates stored ISO-8601 and displayed in local wording; Spanish strings live in the same JSON objects as English (as `{en, es}`) so nothing falls out of sync; pages are static HTML plus one JS file per module, tested at 320 to 430 px.
